fees (intelmarketresearch.com) . Broader definitions, employed by Precedence and SkyQuest, capture

divergence: Mordor Intelligence assigns software platforms 69.65% of total 2025 revenue (Mordor
Intelligence) , while narrower definitions may minimize or exclude this component entirely. When a plat-
form such as Printify charges subscription fees and API access fees, those dollars flow into the software
segment — a segment that now commands roughly two-thirds of all POD value creation.

2.3.2 By End-User: The Business-Creator Divergence
Businesses command 73.88% of POD
revenue in 2025, encompassing corporate branded merchandise, direct-to-consumer (DTC) brands, small
and medium enterprises (SMEs), and promotional products (Mordor Intelligence) . The business seg-
ment’s dominance reflects enterprise demand for zero-inventory fulfillment, seasonal campaign agility,
and brand-building through custom merchandise.
The more strategically significant segment, however, is individual creators — the fastest-growing
end-user category at 27.20% CAGR (Mordor Intelligence) .
Despite representing only ~26% of
current revenue, individual creators are expanding at a rate that will progressively shift the market’s center
of gravity. More than 228,000 active creator stores currently seek zero-stock fulfillment solutions (Mordor
Intelligence) , and the segment’s three-year survival rate of 24% — while seemingly low — actually signals
model resilience in a market where rapid experimentation and iteration are standard (Mordor Intelligence)
. The creator segment’s growth is fueled by social media commerce (TikTok Shop, Instagram Shopping
enabling viral-to-sale conversion), creator-first platform toolkits, and AI-driven design democratization
that allows non-designers to launch full product catalogs within minutes (openPR.com) .
Critically,
over 60% of POD customers prefer buying from small brands, giving creator-driven storefronts a natural
conversion advantage over mass-market designs (Chayaani) .
2.3.3 By Platform Type: Software’s Value Capture
The POD market divides into three platform
archetypes. Software platforms — encompassing API integrations, storefront connectivity tools, SaaS
design platforms, and marketplace middleware — command 69.6% of total revenue (Grand View
Research) (Mordor Intelligence) .
This dominance reflects the reality that the highest-margin, most
scalable value in POD flows to the technology layer that orchestrates transactions between creators,
consumers, and fulfillment providers, not to the physical printing itself.
Service-based fulfillment (end-to-end dropshipping, white-label logistics, custom packaging) repre-
sents approximately 18.4% of the market but is the fastest-growing platform segment at 24.9–28.7%
CAGR (Ecommerce CEO) (Precedence Research) (Grand View Research) , as merchants increasingly
outsource full-stack operations rather than managing fragmented supplier relationships. Marketplaces
— platform-owned storefronts where consumers browse aggregated creator catalogs — account for roughly
12% of revenue but face structural headwinds: rising customer acquisition costs have compelled platforms
such as Redbubble (50% platform fee) and Society6 (5–10% fixed commission) to extract more from cre-
ators, triggering a documented artist exodus that is weakening the marketplace model’s competitive
position.
The segmentation data yields a clear strategic reading for new entrants. Software platform architecture
offers the highest revenue ceiling and the most defensible margin structure.
The creator segment —
though smaller in absolute revenue today — is growing faster than any other end-user category and
aligns with the macro trend toward owned-audience commerce and direct creator monetization. Home
décor offers superior unit economics compared to apparel. Together, these vectors suggest that an AI-
powered platform targeting individual creators with premium home décor and art merchandise capabilities
is positioned at the intersection of the market’s fastest-growing segments.

4. Competitive Landscape — Marketplaces & Niche Players
This chapter examines the demand-side intermediaries: the marketplace platforms that once promised
artists “passive income” through built-in traﬀic, and the secondary and niche players at the edges of
the creator economy. Between 2023 and 2025, the three largest marketplaces—Redbubble, Society6, and
Zazzle—each implemented fee increases or commission cuts so severe that they destroyed the economic
rationale for artists to remain. That collapse, combined with the rise of creator-native alternatives and
Canva’s AI-powered entry, defines the competitive terrain Merch From enters in 2026.
4.1 Marketplace Platforms in Crisis

the artist brings designs and accepts a royalty of 10–20 percent in exchange. In 2024–2025, that bargain
unraveled. Traﬀic stagnated as AI-driven search reduced organic discovery, customer acquisition costs
(CAC) rose across e-commerce, and platforms responded by shifting the burden onto artists.

5. Creator Economics & Pricing Analysis
The print-on-demand (POD) industry’s promise of “no upfront costs” obscures a stark economic reality:
creator take-home pay varies by nearly 12x depending on platform choice. This chapter quantifies the
actual economics creators face across the competitive landscape — the core of Merch From’s value propo-
sition. Where marketplace platforms extract the majority of revenue through layered fees, fulfillment
platforms shift the cost burden to marketing and subscriptions. Understanding this trade-off is essential
for positioning a new entrant.
5.1 Pricing Model Comparison
The industry operates on two fundamentally divergent economic models. The fulfillment-platform
model (exemplified by Printful, Printify, Gelato) gives creators full retail pricing control but requires
them to drive all traﬀic. The marketplace model (Redbubble, Society6, TeePublic, Amazon Merch on
Demand) provides built-in customer traﬀic but extracts revenue through platform fees, fixed royalties, or
revenue-sharing arrangements. Neither model is cost-free — the difference lies in where costs accumulate
and who bears them.
5.1.1 Platform Model: Full Pricing Control with Self-Driven Marketing
Fulfillment platforms
operate on a pay-per-order basis with no mandatory monthly fees. Creators pay a base production cost
per item (ranging from $6.33 for a budget unisex tee on Printify to $11.50 for a Bella + Canvas 3001 on
Printful at list price) and set their own retail markup. (CheckThat.ai) (Printify) This model yields gross
margins of 40–60% per sale, but the creator is solely responsible for customer acquisition, store operations,
and payment processing. Printful charges $8.95–$11.50 for a standard Bella + Canvas 3001 t-shirt, with
shipping adding $4.75 domestically. (Style Factory) Printify’s network of 140+ third-party providers
generally undercuts Printful by 15–30% on base cost — a Gildan 64000 t-shirt costs approximately $6.21
on Printify versus $12.95 on Printful, a $4+ per-unit difference that compounds at volume. (Technavio)
The critical caveat is that fulfillment platforms do not market creator products.
Every sale requires
the creator to bring the customer, typically through paid advertising, social media, or search engine
optimization (SEO).
5.1.2 Marketplace Model: Built-in Traﬀic with Revenue Extraction
Marketplace platforms
handle all aspects of the transaction — traﬀic, payment processing, production, shipping, and customer
service — in exchange for a substantial share of revenue. In 2024–2025, three major marketplaces inde-
pendently overhauled their fee structures in ways that dramatically reduced creator payouts. Redbubble
introduced a tiered fee system in September 2025 under which Standard accounts forfeit 50% of monthly
earnings as a platform fee, plus an additional 50% excess markup fee on any product markup above 20%.
(redbubble.com) Society6 eliminated artist pricing control entirely, fixing royalties at 5% of net sales
for most products and 10% for wall art and paper prints. (Michael Essek) Zazzle applies a Marketing
Royalty Fee of 35–50% on gross royalties depending on product category. (wavespeed.ai)
These changes represent not cyclical adjustments but a structural shift: marketplace platforms can no
longer acquire customers profitably at old royalty rates, and they are passing that cost directly to creators.

7. Fulfillment, Quality & Sustainability
In print-on-demand (POD), the customer never sees the factory—but the factory decides whether the
customer returns. Print quality consistency, delivery speed, and environmental accountability are the
three pillars of operational differentiation in a market where direct-to-garment (DTG) printing technol-
ogy is largely commoditized. For Merch From, which positions around “premium merchandise,” these
operational dimensions are not backend details; they are front-facing brand signals. A misaligned seam
or a plastic mailer can undo premium positioning established by design and marketing.
7.1 Print Quality Rankings
7.1.1 Quality Hierarchy and Consistency Gaps
Print quality in POD is shaped by three vari-
ables: printer hardware, quality-control protocols, and vertical integration. Platforms that own facilities
can enforce standardized calibration and inspection; marketplace platforms routing through third-party
providers inherit variability.
Empirical evidence places Printful at the top of the quality hierarchy. The company has invested $83.7
million in Kornit Atlas MAX and Apollo industrial DTG printers (printondemandbusiness.com) , achiev-
ing color accuracy within 2 Delta-E and a reshipment rate of 0.19%—fewer than 2 in 1,000 orders require
re-production (customcy.com) (Printify) . This rate has remained stable for three years, supported by
a three-step quality-control protocol: blank inspection, in-progress monitoring, and final check before
packaging. Wash-testing on Bella+Canvas 3001 shows approximately 15% color saturation reduction
after 25 cycles with no cracking—within the range end consumers accept (customcy.com) .
Prodigi ranks second for specialized applications, particularly fine art prints, using giclée pigment-based
inks that independent reviewers describe as “the best I’ve seen from any POD” (Ecommerce Platforms) .
Prodigi’s consistency across UK and US labs is high, but the platform does not offer embroidery or the
breadth of apparel customization that Printful does.
